The Vietnam War era shaped American culture through grief, televised violence, draft anxiety, and widespread protest. By 1973, the Paris Peace Accords had been signed, but the emotional effects of the war remained. Many young people had grown up inside a conflict that made authority feel unstable and moral language feel urgent. Anti-war posters, marches, folk music, campus activism, and counterculture communities became ways to resist silence.

The counterculture anti-war movement was not only political. It was also aesthetic and emotional. It asked what kind of life was worth protecting, what kind of future could follow violence, and how art might speak when official language failed. For this project, that context turns the user's personal goodbye into something larger: a threshold shared with a generation that was trying to leave war behind without forgetting what it had cost.
